Nobody quite prepares you for the moment a total stranger starts scrubbing dead skin off your body with what feels like a small piece of sandpaper, and you realize: this is normal here, and also, oh no, it's working. A hammam is a traditional Moroccan bathhouse, and it's not a spa treatment invented for tourists - it's a genuine part of daily life, where locals have gone for centuries to wash, steam, and socialize, long before "self-care" was a marketing term.

Here's what actually happens, what to expect, and how to not embarrass yourself on your first visit.

## What Actually Happens in a Hammam

You'll move through a series of increasingly hot steam rooms to open your pores and soften your skin, followed by the main event: a full-body scrub, usually with a rough exfoliating mitt called a kessa glove, often preceded by a coating of traditional black soap (savon noir) left to sit for a few minutes first. The result is an alarming amount of dead skin coming off your body, which is somehow both mildly horrifying and deeply satisfying to watch. Many sessions finish with rhassoul clay for your hair and skin, and a good coating of argan oil to moisturize everything you just aggressively exfoliated.

## Public Hammam vs. Tourist/Spa Hammam

**Local public hammams** are the real, everyday version - cheap, no-frills, gender-segregated, and used by actual Moroccan families as part of their weekly routine. They're a genuine cultural experience, but don't expect English signage, appointment systems, or anyone doing your scrubbing for you (you typically bring your own products and either self-scrub or pay a small extra fee to have an attendant do it).

**Tourist and spa hammams** - the kind found in riads and dedicated wellness centers - offer a more polished, guided experience: an attendant handles the whole process for you, English is usually spoken, and it's bookable in advance. ## What to Expect, Modesty-Wise

Traditional hammams are gender-segregated, and yes, you'll be undressing more than you might in a Western spa - typically down to underwear, sometimes fully nude in more traditional local settings (norms vary by hammam and gender). Tourist-oriented hammams are generally more accommodating and will provide disposable underwear if you'd rather not go without. If modesty is a real concern, it's completely reasonable to ask about the setup when booking.

## Practical Tips

- **Book ahead for spa-style hammams**, especially in peak season - the good ones fill up fast.
- **Bring flip-flops.** Floors are wet, warm, and not always where you want bare feet.
- **Don't eat a huge meal right before.** Between the heat and the intense exfoliation, a full stomach is not your friend here.
- **Drink water afterward.** You'll sweat more than you expect, and rehydrating makes the "floating on air" feeling last longer instead of turning into a headache.
- **Tip your attendant** if someone scrubbed you personally - it's customary and appreciated, similar to tipping at a spa elsewhere.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a hammam painful?**
Not painful, but the scrubbing is genuinely vigorous - more intense than most people expect from a "spa" experience. Most people find it deeply relaxing once they get used to the sensation.

**Should I go to a local public hammam or a tourist spa hammam for my first time?**
A tourist or riad-based hammam is the easier entry point for first-timers, since someone guides you through the whole process. A local public hammam is worth trying later once you know what to expect.

**How long does a hammam session take?**
A full treatment, including the steam, scrub, and moisturizing, typically runs 45 minutes to over an hour depending on the venue and package.

A hammam isn't a spa day, exactly - it's rougher, more communal, and considerably more Moroccan than that framing suggests. Go in expecting to leave a layer of yourself behind, and come out somehow lighter for it.
